\begin{abstract}
Strassen's rank-7 algorithm for $2\times2$ matrix multiplication gives
recursive arithmetic exponent $\log_2 7 \simeq 2.80735$, while recent
work proves the asymptotic bound $\omega\leq9/4$ over $\mathbb{C}$.
This gap motivates the constructive search for explicit finite
matrix-multiplication algorithms. We present
\texttt{fast\_mm\_search}, a numerical-to-exact procedure that searches
for bilinear decompositions from random coefficients, reconstructs
rational candidates, and verifies them using exact arithmetic. With
only the $2\times2$, rank-7 problem specified, and without supplying
Strassen's coefficients, the search recovered an integer-coefficient
seven-product identity whose factorisation differs from the standard
Strassen formula. Exact rational verification matched all 64
coefficients of the multiplication tensor. The result demonstrates
that a nontrivial exact finite matrix-multiplication algorithm can be
recovered automatically and establishes the procedure for searches at
larger matrix orders and lower recursive exponents.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A rank-$r$ bilinear algorithm for multiplying $u\times u$ matrices
gives recursive arithmetic exponent
\[
\log_u r.
\]
Strassen showed that $2\times2$ matrix multiplication can be computed
with seven scalar products instead of the eight used by direct
multiplication \cite{strassen1969}. His $(u,r)=(2,7)$ construction
therefore gives
\[
\log_2 7 \simeq 2.80735,
\]
establishing that the cubic exponent of direct matrix multiplication
is not optimal.

The matrix-multiplication exponent $\omega$ describes the asymptotic
arithmetic complexity of multiplying square matrices. Recent work
proves
\[
\omega \leq \frac{9}{4}
\]
over $\mathbb{C}$ \cite{openai2026}. This asymptotic result establishes
that substantially lower complexity is possible, while leaving a
constructive question: which explicit finite bilinear decompositions
can realise improved recursive exponents?

We developed \texttt{fast\_mm\_search} to search for finite
decompositions numerically, reconstruct candidate coefficients as
exact rationals, and verify the resulting identities independently.
The classical $(2,7)$ problem provides a controlled validation because
an exact solution is known while its coefficients can be withheld
from the search.

\section{Methods}

For square matrices of order $u$, let
\[
T_u \in \mathbb{Q}^{u^2\times u^2\times u^2}
\]
denote the matrix-multiplication tensor. A rank-$r$ bilinear algorithm
has the decomposition
\[
\widehat{T}_u
=
\sum_{k=1}^{r}
x_k \otimes y_k \otimes z_k,
\]
where $x_k$ and $y_k$ define linear forms in the entries of the two
input matrices and $z_k$ distributes each bilinear product among the
output entries. The dense parameterisation contains $3ru^2$ scalar
coefficients before accounting for term-wise scaling symmetries.

The search separates numerical discovery from exact proof:
\[
\text{random initialisation}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{numerical optimisation}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{rational reconstruction}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{exact verification}.
\]
The validation experiment used $u=2$ and $r=7$, giving 84 raw search
coefficients. Independent random initial values were optimised with
L-BFGS-B using the relative tensor residual
\[
\rho
=
\frac{\lVert T_u-\widehat{T}_u\rVert_F}
     {\lVert T_u\rVert_F}.
\]

Candidates satisfying $\rho<10^{-6}$ were term-normalised and
reconstructed as rational coefficients with denominator bounded by
16. A separate verifier then evaluated the complete multiplication
tensor using exact rational arithmetic. For matrix order $u$, this
checks all $u^6$ tensor coefficients and accepts a candidate only when
every coefficient agrees exactly with $T_u$.

\section{Results}

The blind $(2,7)$ search recovered an exact rank-7
integer-coefficient identity. Before exact reconstruction, the
accepted numerical candidate had relative residual
\[
\rho = 3.093299\times10^{-8}.
\]
Another candidate reached the smaller residual
$1.596061\times10^{-8}$ but did not yield an exactly verified
identity. Numerical proximity therefore identified candidates without
determining exact recoverability.

For
\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
a & b\\
c & d
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
e & f\\
g & h
\end{pmatrix},
\]
the recovered algorithm computes
\[
\begin{aligned}
P_1 &= (b-d)h, &
P_2 &= a(e+f+g+h),\\
P_3 &= (a-b)(g+h), &
P_4 &= (a-c)f,\\
P_5 &= (c-d)e, &
P_6 &= d(e+g),\\
P_7 &= (a-d)(e+g+h),
\end{aligned}
\]
and reconstructs
\[
AB=
\begin{pmatrix}
-P_1-P_3+P_6+P_7 &
P_1+P_2-P_6-P_7\\
P_5+P_6 &
P_2-P_4-P_6-P_7
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Direct expansion gives
\[
AB=
\begin{pmatrix}
ae+bg & af+bh\\
ce+dg & cf+dh
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The independent verifier evaluated all
\[
2^6=64
\]
coefficients of the multiplication tensor using exact rational
arithmetic. All coefficients matched, giving zero mismatches and zero
exact residual.

\section{Discussion}

The recovered identity attains the classical rank-7 bound for
$2\times2$ matrix multiplication. It was obtained from random initial
coefficients without access to Strassen's construction, and its
displayed bilinear products differ from the standard presentation of
Strassen's formula. The result therefore establishes automated
recovery and exact verification of a nontrivial finite
matrix-multiplication algorithm; it does not claim a new rank bound.

Numerical optimisation and exact verification answer different
questions. The smallest floating-point residual observed in the
campaign did not produce the exactly verified identity, showing that
residual magnitude alone cannot establish a bilinear algorithm.
Rational reconstruction followed by coefficient-wise verification
converts a numerical candidate into an exact tensor equality.

For a finite square decomposition, recursive complexity improves on
Strassen whenever
\[
\log_u r < \log_2 7,
\]
equivalently
\[
r < u^{\log_2 7}.
\]
At $u=3$,
\[
3^{\log_2 7} \simeq 21.85,
\]
so $r=21$ is the largest integer rank at this order that would improve
on recursive Strassen:
\[
\log_3 21 \simeq 2.77124 < 2.80735.
\]
The pair $(3,21)$ therefore defines a natural finite search boundary
beyond the $(2,7)$ validation problem.

The corresponding dense parameterisation grows from
\[
3\times7\times2^2 = 84
\]
raw coefficients for $(2,7)$ to
\[
3\times21\times3^2 = 567
\]
for $(3,21)$, while the multiplication tensor grows from
\[
2^6=64
\qquad\text{to}\qquad
3^6=729
\]
coefficients. The increase quantifies the computational step from the
validation problem to a finite target whose exact solution would
already cross the Strassen exponent.

A rank-21 $3\times3$ algorithm would give exponent approximately
$2.77124$, still well above the asymptotic bound $9/4=2.25$. The
$9/4$ theorem therefore supplies an asymptotic benchmark rather than
a claim that a small $(3,21)$ decomposition must exist. The present
result establishes a numerical-to-exact mechanism for searching
finite rules and certifying any recovered identity independently.
